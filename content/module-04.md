# Module 4: Medicines

Medicines are the largest source of errors in hospitals, so NABH has a full chapter on them. This module covers the words used to order a medicine: name, strength, dose, frequency, duration and route. It also covers the medicine categories that NABH watches closely.

### Generic name and brand name

The **generic name** is the name of the active ingredient, for example paracetamol. The **brand name** is the name a company sells it under, for example Dolo or Crocin. Many brands carry the same generic. Hospitals keep a list of the medicines they stock, called a formulary, and doctors prescribe from it.

### Strength, dose, frequency and duration

| Word | Meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Strength | Amount of medicine in one tablet or one ml | 500 mg per tablet |
| Dose | Amount given at one time | 1 tablet, or 500 mg |
| Frequency | How many times in a day | Three times a day |
| Duration | For how many days | 5 days |
| Total daily dose | Dose multiplied by frequency | 500 mg times 3, which is 1500 mg in a day |

To read an order, take it one word at a time. **Inj. Ceftriaxone 1 g IV BD for 5 days** means: an injection of the antibiotic ceftriaxone, 1 gram per dose, given into a vein, twice a day, for five days.

### Routes: how the medicine enters the body

The route is the path a medicine takes into the body. Each route has a short form.

| Route | Short form | How it is given | Common use |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Oral | PO | Swallowed | Tablets, capsules, syrups |
| Sublingual | SL | Under the tongue | Some heart medicines |
| Intravenous | IV | Into a vein | Antibiotics, fluids, emergency drugs |
| Intramuscular | IM | Into a muscle | Many painkillers, vaccines |
| Subcutaneous | SC | Just under the skin | Insulin, blood thinners |
| Intradermal | ID | Into the top layer of skin | Skin tests |
| Topical |  | On the skin | Creams, ointments |
| Inhalation |  | Breathed in | Asthma medicines |
| Rectal | PR | Into the rectum | Suppositories |

### IV and IM compared

These two are the most common injection routes.

|  | IV (Intravenous) | IM (Intramuscular) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Where it goes | Directly into a vein | Into a large muscle, such as the buttock, thigh or upper arm |
| How the nurse does it | Through a cannula, a thin tube left in the vein, or by a direct injection into the vein | A needle pushed into the muscle |
| Speed of action | Fastest. The medicine is in the blood at once | Slower. It is absorbed from the muscle over some time |
| Volume | Can be large, for example a bottle of fluid over several hours | Small, usually a few ml |
| Main risk | A mistake cannot be taken back, since the medicine is already in the blood | Pain, swelling and infection at the site |
| Used when | The patient is very sick, cannot swallow, or needs a fast effect | A medicine needs to act steadily or the patient has no IV line |

SC is a third route. The needle goes just under the skin, and absorption is slower than IM. Insulin is given this way.

### IV fluids

IV fluids are liquids given through a cannula. They replace lost water and salts and carry medicines into the body. The common ones are:

- **NS (Normal Saline):** 0.9 percent salt water
- **RL (Ringer Lactate):** salt water with other body salts
- **DNS (Dextrose Normal Saline):** saline with sugar
- **D5 (5 percent Dextrose):** sugar water

A drip is a slow continuous IV infusion. A bolus is a dose pushed quickly in one go.

### Medicine categories you will hear about

- **Schedule H and H1 drugs:** can be sold only on a doctor's prescription. Schedule H1 includes certain antibiotics, and the pharmacy must keep a register of each sale.
- **Schedule X and narcotic drugs (under the NDPS Act):** strong drugs such as morphine and some sleeping and psychiatric medicines. They need a separate licence, locked storage and a register of every issue.
- **Cold chain medicines:** insulin, vaccines and some injections that must be kept in a fridge at 2 to 8 degrees C. The fridge temperature is recorded every day.
- **LASA drugs (Look Alike Sound Alike):** medicines with similar names or packs, which can be confused. Examples are dopamine and dobutamine, or hydralazine and hydroxyzine. They are stored apart from each other and labelled.
- **High alert medicines:** medicines that can cause serious harm if given wrongly. Examples are insulin, heparin, concentrated potassium, magnesium sulphate, chemotherapy drugs and narcotics. They need extra checks before they are given.

### Safe giving of medicines

Nurses follow the rights of medication. The first five are the right patient, the right drug, the right dose, the right route and the right time. NABH adds more, and we will cover them in Module 8. Before giving any medicine the nurse also checks the patient's allergy status and the expiry date.

A near expiry medicine is one close to its expiry date. The hospital keeps such stock separate and marked so that it is used first or returned before it expires.

### Why this matters for claims

The pharmacy bill is a major part of every claim. The insurer compares it with the doctor's orders and the medication chart. A medicine billed but not ordered, or ordered but not recorded as given, leads to a query.

### Glossary

| --- | --- |
| Generic name | Name of the active ingredient |

| Formulary | List of medicines the hospital stocks |
