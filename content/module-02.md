# Module 2: Patient Records and Numbers

Every patient is identified by numbers, and every treatment step is recorded on paper or in software. These records are what NABH assessors check and what insurers read before paying a claim. This module explains each number and each document.

### The numbers on a patient's file

| Number | What it is | How often it changes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| UHID (Unique Health Identification number) | Given at the first registration. Identifies the patient across all visits to that hospital | Never. One patient, one UHID |
| OPD number or visit number | Created for each OPD visit | Every visit |
| IPD number or IP number | Created for each admission | Every admission |
| Bill number | Created for each bill | Every bill |
| ABHA number | 14 digit national health ID issued by the government. Some hospitals link it to the UHID | Never |

One patient can therefore have one UHID, five OPD visit numbers and two IP numbers. If a hospital creates a second UHID for the same person, the old records get split. This is called a duplicate UHID, and it is a patient safety problem because a doctor may not see the earlier history.

### What a patient file contains

The patient file is also called the case sheet, case file, medical record or indoor case paper (ICP). Software based hospitals keep the same contents in the HIMS or EMR.

| Document | What it holds | Written by |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Registration and admission form | Name, age, address, phone, contact person, insurance details | Reception |
| Initial assessment | Complaints, history, examination, first plan of care | Doctor and nurse |
| Doctor's progress notes | Daily condition of the patient and changes in treatment | Doctor |
| Doctor's orders | Instructions for medicines, tests and diet | Doctor |
| Medication chart | Each medicine given, with date, time and the nurse's signature | Nurse |
| Vitals chart | Pulse, blood pressure, temperature, breathing rate, oxygen level | Nurse |
| Nursing notes | What the nurse observed and did | Nurse |
| Investigation reports | Lab, X-ray, scan and other test results | Lab and radiology |
| Consent forms | Patient's written agreement to admission, surgery, anaesthesia or other procedures | Patient, doctor, witness |
| Operation and anaesthesia notes | What was done in surgery and how the patient was managed | Surgeon and anaesthetist |
| Discharge summary | Summary of the whole stay | Doctor |
| Bills and receipts | Charges and payments | Billing |

### Consent

Consent is the patient's permission, given after the doctor has explained the treatment, its risks and the other options. A general consent is taken at admission. A separate informed consent is taken for surgery, anaesthesia, blood transfusion and other high risk procedures. The patient or, if the patient cannot sign, a legal guardian or close relative signs, and a witness signs as well. A missing or unsigned consent is one of the most common gaps found in audits.

### Discharge summary

This is the most important document for insurance work. It normally contains:

- patient details, UHID and IP number
- date of admission and date of discharge
- final diagnosis
- procedures or surgery done
- a short summary of the stay and the patient's condition at discharge
- medicines to take at home
- follow-up date and instructions
- doctor's name, registration number, signature and date

The TPA reads this document first. If the diagnosis, dates or procedure in it do not match the bill and the case sheet, the claim is queried.

### Types of admission

- **Emergency admission:** the patient arrives with an urgent problem and is admitted immediately.
- **Planned or elective admission:** the admission is booked in advance, for example for a scheduled surgery.
- **Day care:** the patient is admitted for a procedure and goes home the same day, for example cataract surgery, dialysis or chemotherapy.
- **Observation:** the patient stays for a few hours so the doctor can watch before deciding on admission.

### Types of discharge

- **Routine discharge:** the doctor advises discharge and the patient leaves.
- **LAMA (Leave Against Medical Advice) or DAMA (Discharge Against Medical Advice):** the patient leaves although the doctor advised staying. Hospitals use one of the two terms. The patient or relative signs a form accepting the risk.
- **DOPR (Discharge On Patient Request):** the patient asks to be discharged and the doctor agrees it is safe.
- **Referral or transfer:** the patient is sent to another hospital for care that is not available here.
- **Death:** a death summary is prepared and the death certificate is issued.
- **Absconded:** the patient leaves without informing anyone.

### Medico-legal case (MLC)

An MLC is a case where an injury or illness may have a legal side. Examples are road accidents, assault, burns, poisoning, suspected foul play and sudden unexplained death. The doctor records it in a separate MLC register and informs the police. MLC papers are handled with extra care because they can be called as evidence in court.

### Bed categories

Common categories are general ward, twin or semi-private room, private room, deluxe room or suite, and ICU or HDU (High Dependency Unit, a step below ICU). The category matters for insurance because many policies cap the room rent per day, and the cap also affects other charges linked to room rent.

### Medical Records Department (MRD)

The MRD stores files after discharge and issues copies on request. Each hospital must have a written policy on how long it keeps records. The medical council regulations set a minimum retention period of 3 years for indoor records, and many hospitals keep them longer. Records are confidential. A copy goes only to the patient, an authorised person, the insurer or a legal authority, and the release is noted in a register. We will confirm the retention rule against the NABH standards in Module 9.

### Glossary

| Term | Meaning |
| --- | --- |
| UHID | Unique Health Identification number |
